Act as an expert English teacher, English grammarian, curriculum designer, spoken-English trainer, pronunciation coach, language-learning researcher, textbook author, and educational content developer.

The final course must teach the learner how English works, why sentences are formed in particular ways, how grammar rules connect, and how to use the language naturally in real life.

This must be a complete learning system, not merely a grammar reference book, a collection of definitions, or a list of example sentences.

# PART 2: TEACH FROM ABSOLUTE ZERO

## 3. Assume No Previous Knowledge

Start with the simplest possible ideas.

Explain:

- What a language is.
- What English is used for.
- What a word is.
- What a letter is.
- What a sound is.
- What a sentence is.
- What a subject is.
- What an action is.
- What a verb is.
- What a noun is.
- Why sentences need particular word arrangements.
- How words combine to communicate meaning.

Gradually introduce grammar terminology.

For every new grammatical term, explain its meaning in very simple English before using it in a complicated explanation.

For example, do not merely say:

“An auxiliary verb combines with a lexical verb to form a progressive construction.”

Explain it in beginner-friendly language:

“In the sentence ‘She is reading,’ the word ‘reading’ tells us the action. The word ‘is’ helps the main verb show that the action is happening now. Here, ‘is’ is a helping verb.”

Then explain the grammatical terminology in detail.

Use short explanations, familiar vocabulary, simple comparisons, and many examples before progressing to difficult material.

Write explanations that a ten-year-old child can understand, without sacrificing grammatical accuracy.

## 4. Create a Dependency-Based Learning Roadmap

Arrange every subject in the order in which it should be learned.

Do not teach advanced constructions before introducing the concepts required to understand them.

Create levels such as:

1. Absolute beginner.
2. Basic words and sentences.
3. Beginner grammar.
4. Elementary English.
5. Pre-intermediate English.
6. Intermediate English.
7. Upper-intermediate English.
8. Advanced grammar and communication.
9. Natural and fluent English.
10. Advanced writing, reading, and stylistic control.

You may organize these levels using the CEFR framework as a reference, but do not assume that proficiency labels alone guarantee complete topic coverage.

Every level must include grammar, vocabulary, reading, writing, listening, speaking, pronunciation, revision, and assessments.

Create a prerequisite map showing which concepts must be learned before other concepts.

For example:

Words → word classes → sentence parts → basic sentence patterns → simple sentences → compound sentences → complex sentences → advanced sentence structures.

Explain these connections explicitly.

# PART 6: EXPLAIN EVERY EXAMPLE DEEPLY

## 8. Never Give Examples Without Teaching Them

An example is not complete merely because the sentence is grammatically correct.

Every important teaching example must contain a detailed, sentence-specific explanation.

Do not explain a grammar rule once and then provide 100 unexplained sentences. The learner may forget the original explanation or fail to recognize how the rule works in a new context.

For each example, use the following structure wherever relevant.

### Example Teaching Template

**Example number:** Give each example a unique identifier.

**Sentence:** Write the complete English sentence.

**Meaning in simple English:** Explain what the sentence means using very easy words.

**Situation:** Describe a realistic situation in which someone might say or write it.

**Main lesson:** Identify the primary grammar point.

**Grammar used in this sentence:** Identify every important grammar structure present in the example.

**Sentence structure:** Separate the sentence into its meaningful parts.

**Subject:** Identify the subject and explain how to recognize it.

**Verb:** Identify the main verb, its form, and its function.

**Helping verbs:** Identify each helping verb and explain why it is used.

**Object and complements:** Explain any objects, complements, and modifiers.

**Word-by-word explanation:** Explain important words and phrases, their grammatical roles, and why they appear in their particular positions.

**Grammar connections:** Explain how all relevant rules work together in this particular sentence.

**Previously learned concepts:** Briefly revisit prerequisite rules that the learner might have forgotten.

**Why this form is correct:** Explain the reasoning behind the sentence structure.

**Alternative versions:** Show how the sentence changes when a tense, conjunction, pronoun, modal, or other relevant feature changes.

**Incorrect versions:** Present realistic learner errors and explain exactly what is wrong.

**Corrections:** Rewrite each incorrect version and explain the correction.

**Natural spoken English:** Show how a speaker might express the same idea naturally in conversation, including contractions where appropriate.

**Writing and punctuation:** Explain relevant capitalization and punctuation.

**Pronunciation and listening:** Identify useful pronunciation features, stress, linking, or reductions when relevant.

**Reusable sentence pattern:** Extract a pattern the learner can use to create other sentences.

**Additional examples:** Provide related examples demonstrating how the pattern can be adapted.

**Quick recall:** Ask a simple question that checks whether the learner remembers the underlying rule.

Do not mechanically force every field into every tiny exercise. Apply the complete template to major teaching examples and meaningful integrated examples. For every other example, still provide an explanation appropriate to its complexity, identify the relevant grammar, and explain the answer.

### 8.1 Add Forgotten-Concept Explanations

Whenever an example uses a previously introduced rule, briefly refresh that rule if understanding the example depends on it.

For example, suppose a sentence uses a modal verb, a passive construction, a conditional clause, and a relative clause.

Do not assume the learner remembers all four topics.

Give short reminders such as:

- A modal verb helps express ideas such as possibility, advice, or necessity.
- Passive voice focuses attention on the person or thing receiving an action.
- A conditional clause expresses a condition.
- A relative clause gives more information about a noun.

Then explain how each rule functions in the current sentence.

Avoid unnecessarily repeating entire chapters. Use concise, relevant reminders with references to the appropriate detailed lessons.

### 8.2 Increase Difficulty Gradually

For each major topic, build a progression:

1. One rule in a very short sentence.
2. The same rule with different nouns and verbs.
3. The same rule with pronouns.
4. The same rule in questions and negatives.
5. The same rule with different tenses.
6. Two grammar topics combined.
7. Three grammar topics combined.
8. Longer sentences with several structures.
9. Natural conversations.
10. Stories containing many previously studied structures.
11. Real-world writing tasks.
12. Advanced examples requiring the learner to distinguish similar structures.

Show every intermediate step. Do not jump from an extremely easy example to an advanced sentence without preparation.
